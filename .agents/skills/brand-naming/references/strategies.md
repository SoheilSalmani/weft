# Strategies, territories, generation

## The distinctiveness spectrum

This is the spine. USPTO ranks marks from strongest to weakest, and position predicts both registrability and how expensive the name is to defend.

| Class | Example given by USPTO | Strength |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fanciful** | Exxon for petroleum | Strongest, invented, meaning only in relation to the goods |
| **Arbitrary** | Apple for computers | Strong, real word unrelated to the category |
| **Suggestive** | Coppertone for suntan products | Strong, hints at a quality without stating it |
| Descriptive | "Creamy" for yogurt | Weak, registrable only on acquired distinctiveness after years of use |
| Generic | "Bicycle" for bicycles | Cannot function as a trademark |

USPTO: *"the stronger your trademark is, the more easily you can prevent others from using it. Weak trademarks can be difficult and costly for you to defend."*

The practical consequence: **a name optimised for immediate self-explanation is optimised for the weak end.** Clarity belongs in the descriptor.

## Territories before candidates

Derive four to eight conceptual directions, each a different way of thinking about what the thing is. They must be semantically distinct: navigation, memory, precision, compression, cartography, signal, motion, craft, geology, light. Those are illustrations, not a menu.

Generate within each territory. This is what produces genuinely different finalists instead of six suffixed variants of one root.

## Strategies

Coined, arbitrary, suggestive, metaphorical, compound, blend, modified word, classical roots, technical metaphor, action or motion, persona, geographic or mythological, eponym, acronym, category-descriptive.

Two positions worth holding against common practice:

- **Blends are overrated.** Portmanteaus are not automatically memorable and frequently read as machine-generated.
- **Acronyms are weak without prior recognition.** No semantic hook, and they fail the radio test.

## Generation methods

Semantic fields, etymology and roots, metaphor, morphology, compounding, phonetic construction toward a target sound shape, conceptual opposites, verbs of motion, natural and physical phenomena, technical vocabulary from adjacent disciplines.

Never "generate 100 startup names". The pool comes from territories and methods, deliberately.

## Clichés and saturation

Flag, do not ban: `-ify`, `-ly`, `-io`, `-ai`, `-base`, `-flow`, `-hub`, `-stack`, `-labs`, gratuitous X, dropped vowels, two-technical-words compounds, arbitrary Greek and Latin roots.

A strong name may legitimately use one. Mechanical bans produce worse outcomes than judgement. Say when a candidate sits in a saturated pattern and let it compete.

**`AI`, `GPT`, `bot`, `agent`, `copilot` inside a brand name** are close to disqualifying: they date quickly, they collide with platform trademarks, and they name a mechanism rather than a benefit. The category belongs in the descriptor.

**Never build on a famous mark.** `GPTFoo`, `AppleX`, `NotionPlus` create affiliation confusion. The only exception is genuine nominative use for an integration, and even then the brand should be independent.

## Linguistic evidence, used conservatively

What replicates well enough to act on: pronounceability drives processing fluency and downstream favourability; plosive-initial names aid recall; sound symbolism combined with semantic content outperforms either alone.

What does not: the phoneme-to-meaning tables of naming folklore. "K means speed" is not evidence. **Do not write a paragraph analysing every consonant.**

Treat these as tiebreakers between otherwise comparable finalists, never as generators.

Three tests worth applying:

- **Radio test.** Heard once, can it be typed into a search box? Highest-value single test for developer tools.
- **Pronunciation convergence.** Will five people say it the same way?
- **Spelling convergence.** Does the spoken form map to one likely spelling?

Failing these is a cost, not an automatic rejection. Several strong brands fail the spelling test and pay for it with distinctiveness. Decide deliberately.

**Reject "shorter is always better".** Length interacts with distinctiveness: a five-letter dictionary word may be unownable in search while an eight-letter coinage is trivially ownable. Syllables and rhythm matter more than character count, and typing effort matters mainly for CLIs.
